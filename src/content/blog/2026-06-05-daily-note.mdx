This morning I caught myself doing the thing again: opening my phone, seeing a headline, and then immediately checking the reactions to find out what I’m *supposed* to feel.

It’s not just information. It’s instruction.

The narration shows up fast and confident. It tells me what the story “really” is. It tells me who the bad guys are. It hands me a tone — usually some mix of contempt and certainty — and it offers the small reward of belonging if I adopt it quickly.

I keep thinking about how many of these narratives have the same hidden goal: make me easy to steer.

Not in a spooky, conspiratorial way. In the ordinary way incentives work. Outrage is sticky. Certainty travels. If you can turn a complicated moment into a clean identity story, you get a team — and you get me repeating the line before I’ve even formed a thought.

I understand why people reach for that. A lot of us are tired. The ground feels less stable than it used to. When you’re carrying that kind of uncertainty, a simple story can feel like a handrail.

But I’m noticing what the simple story often requires.

It requires a person to become a symbol.

Somebody is no longer a neighbor with a messy mix of motives and limits. They’re “one of those people.” They’re the reason everything is falling apart. The narrative flattens them until they fit inside a shareable sentence.

And once a person is a symbol, you can do almost anything to them in your mind while still feeling righteous.

I don’t want to be naïve about power. People lie. Institutions protect themselves. There are real harms that deserve real attention.

But I’m trying to separate *seeing clearly* from *getting recruited.*

A small practice that helps me is translation.

When I hear a talking point — especially one delivered with that sharp, satisfying edge — I try to translate it back into the human sentence underneath it. Not the polished version meant to win. The tired version.

Sometimes the sentence is: *I don’t feel safe.*

Sometimes it’s: *I work hard and it isn’t adding up.*

Sometimes it’s: *Please don’t make me feel stupid for being confused.*

Those sentences don’t automatically turn into good conclusions, and they don’t excuse harm. But they do change the temperature. They keep me from treating someone else’s fear like entertainment.

I saw a tiny version of this yesterday in a work thread.

Someone posted a screenshot of a confusing workflow. The first replies were the usual heat: “Who designed this?” “How did this ship?” Then one person asked the boring question: what was the user trying to do, and what did the system make likely?

Once we answered that, the story got less thrilling and more accurate. The label assumed context. The primary action didn’t look primary. No villain — just a mismatch between real life and our assumptions. The mood shifted from contempt to repair.

That’s what I want more of right now: fewer performances of certainty, more honest looking.

When I’m not careful, my feed trains me to confuse harshness with intelligence. It trains me to think that staying warm-hearted means I’m not serious.

But the people I trust most aren’t the people with the sharpest takes. They’re the people who can tell the truth without needing to make someone smaller in the process.

So today I’m trying to take a slower step before I repeat the story.

What do I actually know?

What am I guessing?

What feeling is this trying to install in me?

If the answer is “contempt,” I try to wait long enough for my nervous system to come back online. Long enough to remember that I’m not required to turn every event into a posture.

And then I try to do one small thing that’s local enough to be true — a real conversation, a real piece of work, a small act of care — so my whole day doesn’t get spent inside a narrative designed to keep me raw.

The world is going to keep getting narrated at us.

I’m trying to stay close to the people underneath it. Including the person I become when I’m afraid.
