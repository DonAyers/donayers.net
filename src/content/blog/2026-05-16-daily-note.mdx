It’s strange how quickly the world turns into *plot*.

Not just “things happened,” but a neat arc with a villain, a hero, and a little button you can press to prove you belong: share this, mock that, signal you’re on the right side, pick your tribe, stay loud.

I’m not saying nothing is real. A lot of what’s happening is real. Harm is real. Fear is real. People are getting squeezed in ways that are not abstract. But I keep noticing how often the narration arrives pre-packaged — not to help me understand, but to recruit my nervous system.

The stories that travel fastest are the ones that give me an emotion *and* an assignment: be outraged (and be seen), be disgusted (at the correct target), be certain (and don’t ask for more context).

When a story punishes curiosity, it’s rarely trying to tell the truth. It’s trying to maintain the mood.

Underneath a lot of the talking points, I hear people trying to say something simpler and more human: *I don’t feel safe.* *I don’t trust the people in charge.* *I’m tired of being lied to.* *I’m working harder for less.* *I’m scared my kids will have fewer options.* *I don’t know what’s true anymore.*

Those sentences can get translated into all kinds of politics and postures. Sometimes they get translated well. Often they get translated into slogans that make other people into cardboard cutouts. But the sentences themselves are real. And if we can’t hear them, we end up arguing with armor.

I think about this a lot at work.

In healthcare software, when something goes wrong, the quick narrative is moral: a user didn’t follow instructions; a team didn’t think it through; someone messed up. But if we slow down, the deeper story is usually structural: unclear language, brittle handoffs, incentives that reward speed over care, the “edge case” being someone’s real day.

Switching stories — from villain to system — doesn’t excuse harm, but it keeps a human in view and makes the fix more honest.

Public life feels like it’s going the other direction. Villain stories are emotionally efficient: they turn shared discomfort into a clean target and let us skip the slow work of understanding, grieving, and building anything.

Bad-faith negativity loves that. It doesn’t just sell an explanation; it sells a posture: tighten up, get mean, treat other people as props, call it “being realistic.” And if I’m not careful, I can feel myself start to prefer that posture because it feels like protection.

But protection is not the same thing as clarity.

Clarity, in the sense I want, is quieter. It’s the ability to name what’s happening without needing someone else to be humiliated for it to feel true. It’s the ability to say “this matters” without turning my attention into a weapon.

One small practice I’ve been trying is to translate the story I’m being handed into a question that keeps me human.

What is this trying to get me to do? Who benefits from me staying furious and simplified? What fear is this touching in me? What would it cost me to assume there’s a real person under the posture — even if I still disagree, even if I still think something is wrong?

Sometimes the answers don’t make the situation any prettier. Sometimes the answer is still: yes, this is harmful, and we should name it.

But the questions help me notice the line between accountability and entertainment. Between anger that points at reality and anger that just gives my anxiety somewhere to go. Between skepticism and cynicism.

I don’t have a grand theory of where everything is headed. Mostly I’m trying to be faithful to what I can actually touch: the people in my life, the work in front of me, the small choices that shape what kind of person I become while I’m watching.

If the loud narratives right now keep trying to turn fear into certainty, I’m interested in refusing the conversion. Not by checking out — just by staying slow enough to hear what people are really asking for.

Anyway. That’s where I’m at today: trying to keep my hands from moving faster than my heart, and trying to let clarity be something that makes me more precise instead of more cruel.
