I keep noticing how quickly “what’s happening” becomes a demand.

Not just a demand to *care* — I want to care — but a demand to **conclude**. To lock in a take. To pick the villain, pick the tribe, pick the caption that proves I saw the thing the right way.

A lot of narratives right now don’t feel like someone offering perspective. They feel like someone trying to seize my steering wheel.

You can feel it in the pacing. A clip, a screenshot, a headline with a tone already baked in. The story isn’t asking me to understand; it’s asking me to react. And the reaction it wants most isn’t even anger — it’s *contempt*. Contempt is tidy. Contempt is fast. Contempt makes a complicated world feel like a simple sorting problem.

I don’t think this is only about politics or culture wars or “the internet.” I think it’s about people being squeezed.

When life feels expensive and uncertain, when institutions feel wobbly, when the future looks less like a plan and more like a guessing game, your nervous system starts hunting for something solid. Certainty is a kind of painkiller. Even if it’s counterfeit. Even if it requires turning strangers into cardboard cutouts.

I’ve been trying to watch the moment right before my mind snaps into that shape.

It’s usually subtle. A little heat in my chest. A little “of course.” A little itch to say, *see?* And then, without noticing, I’m no longer curious. I’m just recruiting evidence.

What helps me is to translate the talking point back into a human sentence.

When someone is shouting a slogan, sharing a dunk, forwarding the most extreme version of a story, what might they be trying to say underneath the armor?

Sometimes it sounds like: I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel heard. I don’t trust the people in charge. I’m tired of being manipulated. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I’m scared my kids won’t have a fair shot. I don’t know who’s telling the truth.

Those sentences don’t automatically produce good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. But they’re closer to the root than the performance.

I think about this a lot at work, because I see how “narratives” form in miniature.

A bug gets reported. A patient-facing workflow goes sideways. The first story that shows up is usually personal: someone did something dumb, someone didn’t care, someone dropped the ball. It’s emotionally efficient. It gives you a culprit and a clean direction for your irritation.

But if you slow down, the story that fixes the problem is almost always structural. The text was ambiguous. The handoff assumed context. The UI made the wrong thing the easiest thing. The “edge case” wasn’t an edge at all — it was somebody’s real day, under stress, trying to do the right thing quickly.

When I remember that, I don’t get softer about accountability. I get more accurate. And the accuracy tends to be kinder, because it keeps a person in view.

Public life right now seems addicted to the opposite trade: less accuracy, more heat.

Bad-faith negativity thrives when we confuse intensity for insight. It trains us to treat cruelty like honesty. It rewards the posture of “I’m not naïve” as if being hard is the same thing as being clear.

If I’m honest, I understand the temptation. Cynicism can feel like armor. If I expect the worst, I won’t be surprised. If I stay sarcastic, I don’t have to admit I’m scared. If I keep people small in my head, I don’t have to wrestle with the grief of how complicated it is to be human together.

But cynicism isn’t clarity. It’s just a way of protecting yourself by shrinking your capacity to care.

So today my little practice is this: when a story arrives with a ready-made enemy, I try to ask what it’s doing to me before I let it move my hands.

Is it making me more precise, or just more certain?

Is it helping me see people, or helping me enjoy not seeing them?

I don’t have a grand answer for where the world is headed. Mostly I’m trying to keep a steadier attention — one that can name what’s real without needing a target to burn.

Reality is still made of people with nervous systems. I’m trying to live like that’s true, even when the loudest stories are begging me to forget it.
