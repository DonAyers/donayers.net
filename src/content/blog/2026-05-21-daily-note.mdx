This week has had that familiar vibe: everything is supposedly *the* thing.

The headline isn’t just information — it arrives with an instruction manual. Here’s what it proves. Here’s who did it. Here’s what kind of person you are if you don’t repost it with the correct tone.

I don’t think that’s an accident. A lot of the narratives moving fastest right now feel less like attempts to understand reality and more like attempts to *recruit* me. They’re optimized for the part of my brain that wants simple edges: us / them, safe / dangerous, smart / stupid, good / evil. If the story can get me there quickly, it can keep me there. It can rent out my attention.

The thing I’m trying to notice is what that kind of story does to my body.

It tightens me. It speeds me up. It makes my curiosity feel like a liability. And it offers a cheap substitute: certainty.

Sometimes the certainty is wrapped in righteousness. Sometimes it’s wrapped in “I’m just being realistic.” But either way, the emotional transaction is the same. If I accept this narrative as-is, I don’t have to sit with the harder truth: a lot of people are scared, and the systems we rely on feel less trustworthy than we want them to be.

When life gets expensive, when the future feels like a moving target, when institutions look like they’re improvising, the nervous system starts negotiating. It asks for anything that will make the room feel less shaky. A clean villain can do that. So can a clean savior. So can a clean explanation for why the world feels off.

I’ve been thinking about that because I see a small version of it at work.

When something goes wrong in healthcare software, the first story is almost always personal. Somebody messed up. Somebody didn’t care. Somebody wasn’t paying attention.

It’s emotionally satisfying — until you actually have to fix the thing. And the fix almost never lives inside a single person’s character. It lives in the system: ambiguous language, missing guardrails, a workflow that assumes too much context, an “edge case” that’s actually a normal day for a tired human trying to do the right thing quickly.

That’s why I’ve come to love blameless postmortems. Not because accountability doesn’t matter, but because accuracy matters more. If you want less harm, you have to be willing to look for root causes instead of emotional convenience. You have to be willing to keep a person in view while still telling the truth.

The world feels like it’s losing that muscle.

So much public narration right now is engineered to turn anxiety into contempt. It tells you that being kind is being naïve, that being curious is being weak, that being measured is being complicit. It tries to make “calm” sound like “not caring.”

But calm is not the absence of conviction. It’s just what happens when you refuse to let fear choose your words for you.

I’m not pretending I’m above it. I can feel the pull. The moment I read a story and my fingers want to share it before my heart even catches up — that’s the hook setting.

When I catch that moment, I’m trying a small translation exercise: what is the human sentence underneath this?

Not the slogan. Not the dunk. Not the worst-faith interpretation.

The human sentence is usually something like: I don’t feel safe. I feel like the rules keep changing. I’m tired of being manipulated. I’m worried about my kids. I’m working harder and it’s still not enough. I don’t know who’s telling the truth.

Those sentences don’t automatically turn into good policy, good strategy, or good behavior. They don’t excuse harm. But they do keep me anchored in reality: a world made of people with nervous systems.

And once I remember that, a different set of questions opens up.

Who is this narrative asking me to stop seeing?

What part of me is it trying to inflame?

Does it make me more accurate, or just more certain?

Does it move me toward anything repairable, or does it only offer the temporary thrill of being right?

I don’t have a tidy conclusion about where everything is headed. Mostly I’m trying to protect the part of myself that still believes people can change — including me.

Today that looks like staying slower than the feed, speaking in full sentences, and treating “I don’t know yet” as a valid place to stand. Not forever. Just long enough to keep my clarity from turning into a weapon.
