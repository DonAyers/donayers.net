Last night I noticed something I’ve been doing more lately: I’ll read a piece of news, then immediately scan the reactions before I’ve even formed a thought.

Not because I’m trying to outsource my opinions, exactly. More because there’s a subtle threat in the air: *don’t be late.* Don’t be the last one to know which tone is acceptable. Don’t miss the moment when the group decided what this means.

That’s one of the strange features of public life right now. Events still happen, of course, but the bigger thing that happens is the narration. The narration arrives with instructions.

Here’s what to fear.

Here’s who to blame.

Here’s what kind of person you are if you hesitate.

And the narration is almost always faster than the part of me that can actually tell the truth.

I don’t mean “the truth” as in a perfect neutral view from nowhere. I mean the more modest thing: the honest sentence about what I can actually see, what I don’t know yet, and what my body is doing while I’m taking it in.

The stories that spread the fastest don’t ask for that. They ask for a posture.

A posture is useful because it turns uncertainty into belonging. If I take the posture, I get to be on a team. I get a script. I get a little hit of relief from the loneliness of not knowing what’s going to happen next. And when I’m tired, that relief is incredibly tempting.

But there’s a cost.

Postures make people smaller. They compress real human motives into a caricature that can be traded like a token. Someone becomes “one of those people,” and now the conversation doesn’t have to touch grief, or fear, or shame, or the unbearable feeling that your effort isn’t being met with anything like security.

I keep coming back to the same suspicion: a lot of the ugliness in the air is a failed attempt to ask for dignity.

Not always. Some people are just being cruel.

But so often, underneath the rhetoric, I can hear a normal human request being made badly. *Please stop treating me like I’m disposable.* *Please admit this is hard.* *Please tell me I’m not crazy for feeling like the ground is shifting.*

If that’s even partially true, it changes how I want to hold the moment.

It doesn’t mean I accept misinformation or excuse harm. It means I try to keep the person in view even while I disagree with what they’re doing. It means I try not to let my own fear rent out my attention to the nearest simplified story.

I see the same pattern in software teams.

When something goes wrong, the quickest explanation is usually a character judgment: someone was careless, someone didn’t think, someone didn’t care. That story travels fast because it feels clean. It gives you an outlet.

But if you actually want fewer outages and fewer repeat mistakes, you end up doing something less satisfying: you go looking for context. You look at what the system made likely. You look at incentives and handoffs and defaults and sleep and time pressure. You hold multiple truths at once. It’s slower, but it produces repair instead of heat.

I think we’re losing the taste for that kind of slowness.

So today I’m practicing a tiny form of resistance that feels almost silly to write down: before I share the hot take, I try to say a full sentence to myself. What is the claim here? What do I actually know? What am I guessing? What feeling is this trying to install in me?

If I can’t answer those questions, I try to delay. Not forever. Just long enough for my nervous system to come back online.

And then I try to do something grounding that isn’t performative: a real conversation, a real task, a small act of care that’s local enough to be true.

Maybe that’s the simplest version of what I want right now: clarity without contempt. Seriousness without spectacle. The ability to name what’s wrong without needing my heart to get harder in order to prove I’m paying attention.

The stories will keep spinning. I’m trying to stay close to the people underneath them — including the person I become when I’m afraid.
