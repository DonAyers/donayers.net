I’ve been noticing how many stories arrive pre-assembled now.

Not just “here’s what happened,” but “here’s what it means,” and “here’s who you should be mad at,” and “here’s the tone you need to use so your people recognize you.” The headline comes with a little badge you’re supposed to pin on your chest.

I don’t think that’s only a media problem. I think it’s an exhaustion problem.

When the world feels unstable — money tight, trust thin, the future shifting — my nervous system hunts for something solid. A clean story can feel like a handrail, even when it’s counterfeit.

That’s the narrative trick I keep seeing: take real fear and convert it into a posture.

The posture might look like contempt. It might look like certainty. It might look like “I’m just being realistic.” But the emotional transaction is the same: if I accept this framing, I don’t have to sit in the messy middle where multiple things are true at once. I don’t have to admit I’m unsure. I don’t have to feel how vulnerable it is to care.

And once I’m recruited into the posture, my attention is easier to steer.

I can feel it in my body: breathing shallow, mind sharpening. The goal shifts from understanding to winning, and I start rehearsing a performance instead of looking for the truth.

That’s usually the moment I’m furthest from clarity.

Clarity doesn’t feel like a clenched jaw. It feels like holding a real sentence without needing it to be a slogan. It can say, “This is harmful,” without needing humiliation as proof.

I’ve learned this most concretely at work, because software is brutally honest about stories.

When something breaks in a healthcare workflow, the fastest explanation is personal: a user messed up, someone didn’t care, someone was incompetent. That story moves quickly because it’s emotionally satisfying. It gives you a culprit and a release valve.

But if you actually want the harm to stop happening, you have to get slower.

You have to look at the system: the wording, the defaults, the missing guardrails, the way stress changes what’s “obvious.”

In other words: the blame story is fast, and the root-cause story is slow.

Public life right now seems to reward the fast story. The version that fits in a clip and recruits you into a side. A lot of people are just scared and tired, but the incentives still push everything toward heat.

So I’m trying a small practice, mostly for my own sanity.

When I feel a narrative trying to recruit me, I try to translate it into the human sentence underneath it. Not the talking point. Not the clever cruelty. The human sentence.

Sometimes it sounds like: I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel respected. I’m tired of being manipulated. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I don’t know who to trust.

Those sentences don’t automatically produce good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. But they do keep me anchored in the real material the world is made of: people with nervous systems.

And once I’m anchored, I can ask a better question than “Which side am I on?”

I can ask: what is this story doing to me? Is it making me more accurate, or just more certain? Is it helping me see people, or helping me enjoy not seeing them? Is it pointing toward something repairable, or is it only offering the temporary thrill of being right?

I’m not trying to opt out of reality. I’m trying to stay in it — without letting my fear rent out my attention to whoever can spike my heart rate the fastest.

Today that looks like taking a beat before I share something, speaking in full sentences, and treating “I might be missing something” as a normal human stance instead of a failure.

The world is loud. A lot of the narration is designed to keep us loud too.

I’d like to stay a little quieter — not as an aesthetic, but as a way of keeping a person in view.
