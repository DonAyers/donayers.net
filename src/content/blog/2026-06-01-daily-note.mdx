June showed up quietly.

I made coffee, opened the news, and felt that familiar tug: the world is happening, and the narration is already running ahead of it.

That’s the part that gets me lately. It’s not just *what* happened — it’s the packaged meaning that arrives with it. The ready-made tone. The invitation to join a side. The subtle pressure to prove you’re not naive, not late, not “one of them.”

A lot of the loudest stories aren’t really trying to inform me. They’re trying to recruit me. They’re trying to install a reflex.

Fear. Contempt. A thin kind of triumph.

Those feelings are efficient. They make the world feel legible fast. They also turn people into symbols.

Once someone becomes a symbol — a type, an avatar, a punchline — the conversation gets easier. You don’t have to deal with the fact that real human beings are contradictory and tender and scared and proud. You can just argue over the symbol.

I don’t think most of us are choosing that on purpose. I think we’re tired.

Everything costs more than it should. Trust feels thinner. The future feels less like a plan and more like a weather system. When my nervous system is already running hot, a clean narrative can feel like relief — like a handrail.

But clean narratives come with a tax: they make it normal to practice contempt.

Contempt is a shortcut. It lets me feel clear without actually becoming more accurate. It makes me feel protected without making anything safer.

So I’ve been asking a question that slows me down:

What is the human sentence under this?

Not the talking point. Not the dunk. The human sentence.

Sometimes I can’t find one, and that’s information too. But often, if I listen carefully, I hear something like: *I don’t feel safe.* *I don’t trust anyone to tell me the truth.* *I’m working hard and it isn’t adding up.* *Please don’t make me feel stupid for being confused.*

Those sentences don’t excuse harm. They don’t automatically produce good ideas. But they change the kind of attention I bring.

If I can hear the human sentence, I’m less likely to get pulled into the performance of certainty. I’m more likely to stay in contact with the reality that public life is made of: people with nervous systems.

I think about this at work because software is merciless about stories.

When something breaks, the first story is usually personal: somebody didn’t care, somebody wasn’t paying attention, that team always does this. It travels fast because it feels clean.

But if you want fewer repeat failures, you end up telling a different kind of story. You go looking for context. You ask what the system made likely — especially for a tired human on a busy day. The fix is usually quieter than the blame.

Public life right now feels like it’s losing patience for that kind of quiet.

Everything wants to be immediate. Everything wants to be a test. Everything wants to turn “are you paying attention?” into “prove you’re on the right team.”

I can feel my own heart react to that. I can feel it want to armor up.

So today I’m practicing a small, almost boring kind of resistance.

Before I share the take — before I even fully *adopt* the take — I try to say one honest sentence to myself: what do I actually know here?

And then: what am I guessing?

And then: what feeling is this story trying to install in me?

If the answer is “panic” or “contempt,” I try to wait. Not forever. Just long enough to let my body settle back into something like choice.

Then I try to do something local enough to be true: send the message I’ve been putting off, do a real piece of work, take a walk, have a conversation that isn’t about scoring points.

None of that fixes the world. It does, however, change the kind of person I’m becoming while the world is being narrated at me.

I don’t have a tidy answer for where we’re headed. Mostly I’m trying to hold onto something like: clarity without cruelty. Seriousness without spectacle. The ability to name what’s harmful without needing my heart to get harder as proof that I noticed.

The narration will keep spinning.

I’m trying to stay close to the people underneath it — including the person I become when I’m afraid.
