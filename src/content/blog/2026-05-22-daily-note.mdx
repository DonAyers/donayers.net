Something I keep feeling lately is *compression*.

Not just the usual “the news is a lot.” More like: everything gets squeezed into a shape that can travel fast.

A complicated situation becomes a clip. A clip becomes a verdict. A verdict becomes an identity test. If you don’t share it with the right tone, you’re suspect. If you ask for context, you’re stalling. If you feel sad instead of hot, you’re not paying attention.

That’s the part that makes my shoulders rise.

Because a lot of the stories moving fastest right now aren’t mainly about understanding. They’re about *settling the nervous system* — by offering a clean explanation and a clean enemy. The world is confusing, so here’s a simple handle. The future feels shaky, so here’s someone you can blame. You feel small, so here’s a posture that will make you feel powerful for twenty seconds.

I get why it works. Life feels tight for a lot of people. Money. Time. Trust. The sense that the “normal” we were promised keeps drifting further away. When you live inside that kind of strain, ambiguity isn’t just intellectually annoying — it can feel physically unsafe. Certainty, even counterfeit certainty, can feel like a handrail.

The trouble is what that certainty costs.

The compressed narrative usually asks me to stop seeing a person and start seeing a symbol. It asks me to enjoy not being curious. It asks me to turn fear into contempt and then call it realism.

I’ve been thinking about this in a smaller, more ordinary way at work.

When a bug shows up, it’s amazing how quickly a team can invent a story that feels satisfying and wrong. *Who messed up?* *Why didn’t they think about this?* *How did we let that happen?* Those questions feel like motion. They feel like control. And sometimes they’re not even malicious — they’re just what the brain does when it’s trying to find something solid.

But when we actually fix the problem, the story usually changes. The fix lives in the unglamorous details: a label that implies the wrong thing, a workflow that assumes too much context, a default that makes the unsafe path the easiest path, a handoff that doesn’t survive a tired human on a busy day.

In other words: the “villain story” is fast, and the “root cause story” is slow.

Public life right now seems to reward the fast story almost exclusively. Not because everyone is evil or dumb, but because the incentives are brutal. The platforms reward heat. The commentators reward certainty. The group chats reward the joke that lands. And a lot of us are walking around depleted — which makes us easier to recruit.

When I notice myself getting pulled into that current, I’m trying a small translation exercise.

When someone is broadcasting a talking point like it’s the whole truth, what might they be trying to say underneath it?

Sometimes the human sentence sounds like: I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel respected. I’m tired of being manipulated. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I don’t know who is telling the truth. I want someone to admit this is scary.

Those sentences don’t automatically lead to good conclusions. They don’t excuse harm. But they do help me stay anchored in reality: a world made of people with nervous systems.

And once I’m anchored, I can ask better questions — not to perform intelligence, but to protect my own humanity.

Is this story making me more accurate, or just more certain?

Is it helping me see what’s real, or helping me enjoy being disgusted?

Is it pointing toward something repairable, or is it only offering the temporary thrill of being right?

I don’t have a grand theory of where everything is headed. Mostly I’m trying to stay slower than the feed and closer to actual people. I’m trying to let my attention be something I *give* on purpose, not something that gets rented out by whoever can spike my heart rate the fastest.

Today that looks like speaking in full sentences, holding my conclusions with a little more humility, and remembering that “I don’t know yet” is not a moral failure.

It’s just a way of making room for the root cause story — the one that keeps a person in view.
