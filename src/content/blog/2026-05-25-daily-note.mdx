This week I’ve felt that familiar pressure to pick a script.

Something happens somewhere in the world, and before I’ve even had time to understand it, I’m offered a complete package: what it *means*, who it *proves* is evil or stupid, what kind of person I have to be to be on the right side of it. The story doesn’t just want my opinion — it wants my posture.

I don’t think that’s only about politics or “the media.” It’s also about how tired people are.

When life feels expensive and unstable, uncertainty stops being an abstract concept and starts feeling like a threat. And when my nervous system is keyed up, a clean narrative feels like relief. It gives me a handhold. It lets me turn a complicated situation into a quick verdict, and a quick verdict into belonging.

But I keep noticing what that relief costs.

A lot of the loud narratives right now are built like loyalty tests. They don’t invite you to see. They invite you to *signal*. They reward speed, heat, and contempt. They train you to treat curiosity as weakness and humility as betrayal. They make it feel normal to flatten entire groups of people into symbols, because symbols are easier to punish than neighbors.

When I’m honest, I can feel the recruitment in my body. My jaw tightens. My thoughts get sharp and fast. I start composing replies in my head. I don’t feel *clear* — I feel armed.

And then I remember something from work.

In healthcare software, the first story about a problem is almost never the best one. A ticket comes in and the fast narrative is personal: the user didn’t read, somebody messed up, someone on another team is careless. That story moves quickly because it’s emotionally satisfying. It gives you a culprit and a place to put your frustration.

But if you actually want the harm to stop repeating, you have to get slower. You have to ask what the system made likely: the wording, the defaults, the missing guardrails, the handoff that only works when everyone is rested and has perfect context. The “edge case” is usually just a tired human having a normal day.

The blame story is fast. The root-cause story is slow.

Public life seems to reward the fast story.

So I’ve been trying a small translation practice.

When I run into a narrative that makes me instantly certain, I try to translate it from a verdict into a request. Underneath the posture, what is the human sentence?

Not the meme. Not the sneer. The human sentence.

A lot of the time it’s: I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel respected. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I don’t know who to trust. I want someone to admit this is scary.

Those sentences don’t automatically produce good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. But they do change what I’m arguing with. They bring me back to the real material the world is made of: people with nervous systems.

And once I’m back there, I can ask questions that actually help.

Is this story making me more accurate, or just more certain?

Is it inviting me to build anything, repair anything, understand anything — or is it only offering the temporary thrill of being right at someone?

I don’t have a neat answer for how to live inside the current level of noise. But I’m trying to hold onto one distinction that matters to me: clarity versus performance.

Performance is what happens when I’m optimizing for belonging. Clarity is what happens when I’m willing to be a little slower than the feed, and a little more honest than the script.

Today, my best practice looks boring on purpose. Fewer hot takes. More full sentences. More “I might be missing something.” More attention to the people in front of me, and less to the imaginary audience in my pocket.

The world is loud, and the narration is trying to make me loud too.

I’d like to stay a little quieter — not as an aesthetic, and not as avoidance, but as a way of keeping a person in view. Including myself.
