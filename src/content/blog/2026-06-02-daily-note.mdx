This morning I noticed how quickly my brain tries to turn the world into a story with a villain.

I wasn’t even fully awake yet. Coffee, a little light in the kitchen, my phone doing the thing it does — handing me a stream of “what this means” before I’ve had any chance to feel what actually happened. I could feel the pull toward the fast version of reality: the version where everything is obvious, everyone is a type, and the only responsible move is to pick a side and harden.

It’s strange, because the events themselves are often complicated and slow. The *narration* is instant.

The narration shows up with pre-made emotions. It comes with a posture I’m supposed to adopt so my people recognize me. It quietly suggests that hesitation equals complicity, that uncertainty equals stupidity, that asking for context is just a cover for not caring.

And I get why that works.

A lot of us are carrying too much. Money is tight. Attention is scattered. Trust feels thin. The future keeps refusing to be predictable. When my nervous system is already overloaded, a clean narrative can feel like relief — a handrail that says, “at least I know what’s going on.”

But I’m starting to see the trade more clearly.

The cleanest stories are rarely trying to make me more accurate. They’re trying to make me more *certain*. Certainty is soothing. It also has a price: it almost always requires an enemy.

Once there’s an enemy, the tone changes. People become symbols. The conversation stops being about what’s true and starts being about what identity I’m performing. The goal becomes staying pure, staying sharp, staying ahead of whoever is trying to trick me.

I’ve been thinking about this through the lens of my own work, because software doesn’t let you hide from story-making for long.

When something breaks in a healthcare workflow, the first story that shows up is usually personal and moral: someone didn’t care, someone was sloppy, that team always does this. It’s emotionally efficient. It gives you a culprit and a brief hit of righteousness.

And then, if you actually want the harm to stop repeating, you have to tell a different story.

You have to get slower. You have to look at the system: the defaults that nudged someone into a mistake, the ambiguous label, the missing guardrail, the way stress changes what feels “obvious.” In other words, you move from blame to context. From heat to repair.

Public life right now feels like it’s being pulled in the opposite direction.

So many stories are optimized for speed and loyalty. They don’t just describe what happened — they try to install a reflex in me. Fear. Contempt. Triumphant disgust. The feeling that I’m one of the smart ones because I already know how to interpret this.

When I’m honest, that’s the part I have to watch: how much I enjoy the posture.

Because the posture feels like safety, but it’s usually just armor. And armor has its own gravity. If I wear it long enough, I start to forget what it was protecting.

Lately I’ve been practicing a small translation exercise, mostly as a way of staying human while the feed tries to recruit me.

When I feel a story tightening my chest — when I feel the urge to share it with a little extra bite — I try to ask: what is the human sentence underneath this?

Not the slogan. Not the dunk. The sentence a tired person might be reaching for.

Sometimes it’s something like: *I don’t feel safe.* Or: *I’m tired of being lied to.* Or: *I work hard and it isn’t adding up.* Or: *Please don’t make me feel stupid for being confused.*

Those sentences don’t magically turn into good policy. They don’t excuse cruelty. But they help me see the root material the world is made of: people with nervous systems, trying to protect something.

And once I can see that, I can do something more honest than picking a side on command.

I can hold two things at once: that harm is real, and that a lot of the noise is scared people trying to translate fear into control. That anger can be justified, and that contempt is a tempting shortcut that doesn’t actually make me clearer.

Today, my best attempt at staying grounded looks very plain.

I’m trying to speak in full sentences. I’m trying to admit what I don’t know before I broadcast what I feel. I’m trying to do at least one thing that’s local enough to be true — a real task, a real conversation, a small act of care that doesn’t require an audience.

Not because the big world doesn’t matter, but because this is how I keep my heart from turning into a weapon while I’m watching it.

The narration will keep spinning.

I’m trying to keep choosing clarity that doesn’t require cruelty — and attention that can still see a person, even when the story is begging me not to.
