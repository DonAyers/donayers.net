I’ve been thinking about how quickly “what’s going on” turns into “who’s to blame.”

Not just in the obvious places. Everywhere. A story shows up and it’s already carrying a posture: here’s the villain, here’s the team, here’s the emotion you should perform so people know you belong.

And I get why that posture is tempting.

The world doesn’t feel steady. Money is tight, trust is thin, and a lot of us are tired. When my nervous system is running hot, a clean narrative feels like relief — a handrail.

The problem is what that relief costs.

The fastest narratives are usually the flattest. They compress a complicated situation into something that travels: a clip, a slogan, a verdict. They don’t just tell you what happened — they tell you who to despise and what tone to use.

I can feel that kind of story trying to recruit me. My jaw tightens, my mind starts sharpening, and curiosity starts to feel like a liability. The goal shifts from understanding to securing my footing by becoming certain, fast.

I don’t think that’s just a personal failing. It’s a predictable response to living inside systems that feel brittle. When the room feels shaky, the brain looks for anything that reduces ambiguity.

Bad-faith negativity knows this. It doesn’t need a careful argument; it just needs to spike my heart rate and offer me a target. It teaches me to treat contempt as clarity, and to call the temporary thrill of being “above” someone a kind of wisdom.

When I notice myself slipping into that, I’ve been trying a small translation exercise.

What is the human sentence underneath this narrative?

Not the talking point. Not the worst-faith interpretation. Not the joke that lands in the group chat. The human sentence.

A lot of the time it’s something like: I don’t feel safe. I’m tired of being lied to. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I don’t know who’s telling the truth. I want somebody to admit this is scary.

Those sentences don’t automatically produce good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. But they bring me back to the actual material the world is made of: people with nervous systems.

And once I’m back there, I can ask better questions.

Is this story making me more accurate, or just more certain?

Does it move me toward anything repairable, or does it just offer the cheap comfort of a clean enemy?

I think about this at work because software is brutally honest about stories. When something breaks, the quick explanation is personal: someone was careless, someone didn’t think, the other team dropped the ball. But if you want the harm to stop repeating, you end up looking at the system: wording, defaults, missing guardrails, handoffs that don’t survive a tired human on a busy day. The fix is quieter than the blame.

Public life right now feels like it’s losing patience for that kind of quiet.

Everything wants to be immediate. Everything wants to be a test.

So I’m practicing something small, mostly for my own sanity: I’m trying to keep “I might be missing something” as a normal sentence. Not as a way of dodging reality, but as a way of staying in it. I’m trying to stay slower than the feed and closer to actual people.

If you’re feeling overwhelmed, I don’t have a tidy answer. I’m not even sure “answer” is the right shape for what we need.

But I do think it matters what we let our fear turn us into.

I want my clarity to cost less — less contempt, less certainty-as-armor, less turning other people into symbols so I can feel stable for a minute. I want to be able to name what’s harmful without making my heart harder as proof that I’m serious.

The world is loud. A lot of the narration is designed to keep us loud too.

Today I’m trying to stay a little quieter — not as an aesthetic, but as a way of keeping a person in view.
