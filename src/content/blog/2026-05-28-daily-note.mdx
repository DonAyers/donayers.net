This week I’ve been noticing how often the world tries to speak *for* me before I’ve even had a chance to notice what I actually feel.

A headline lands and it’s not just information — it’s an invitation to adopt a stance. A friend sends a link and there’s a pressure tucked inside it: *say the line so I know you’re with me.*

A lot of it is framed like it’s about truth, but it’s really about belonging.

The narratives spinning right now are built to travel. They’re quick, emotionally hot, and they sort people fast. They don’t just tell you what happened — they tell you who you’re allowed to be.

I don’t think they win because they’re accurate. I think they win because we’re tired.

When money feels tight and the future feels harder to picture, the nervous system starts bargaining for certainty. Uncertainty doesn’t just feel inconvenient — it can feel like danger. A clean story can feel like a handrail, even when it’s a bad one.

I can tell when I’m being recruited because my body changes before my mind catches up. Jaw tight. Shoulders up. Thoughts speeding past my actual experience. I stop looking and start preparing a performance.

Bad-faith negativity loves that state. It doesn’t need to make me wiser — it just needs to make me *sure*. Certainty is easy to monetize. Contempt is easy to share.

So I keep coming back to a quieter question: what is someone actually trying to say underneath all this?

Not the talking point. Not the dunk. The human sentence.

Often it’s something plain: I’m scared I’m falling behind. I don’t trust the people in charge. I feel like the rules changed and nobody told me. I want someone to admit this is hard. I don’t want to be treated like I’m invisible.

Those sentences don’t magically produce good ideas, and they don’t excuse harm. But they put me back in contact with the real material the world is made of: people with nervous systems, trying to protect what they love.

I’ve learned this most concretely in software.

A bug report can arrive like an accusation — “This is broken,” “this is ridiculous,” “who shipped this?” If I take it personally, I can answer from the same place: defensiveness and cold competence.

But when I translate the message into the need underneath it, the situation becomes workable. The sentence is usually: I’m under pressure and I can’t afford surprises. I’m afraid I’m going to lose work. I feel stupid and I hate that feeling. I need this to be reliable.

Public life right now seems to reward the opposite discipline. The fastest interpretation gets treated like the truest one. The harshest tone gets treated like the most serious one. Humiliation gets treated like proof.

I don’t have a grand solution for that. I mostly just have a small practice I’m trying to keep.

When a story tries to recruit me, I try to notice what it’s doing to me.

Is it making me more accurate, or just more certain?

Is it helping me see a whole person, or turning them into a symbol?

And if I can’t answer those questions, I try to do something very unheroic: I stay slower than the feed. I speak in full sentences. I let “I might be missing something” be a normal stance instead of a failure.

Not because the stakes are low. Because the stakes are high, and I don’t want my seriousness to be measured by how hard my heart gets.

The world is going to keep offering me simple stories. Today I’m trying to meet them with a little more patience — for other people, and for myself — and keep the conversation close to the ground: real fear, real dignity, and whatever repair is actually possible.
