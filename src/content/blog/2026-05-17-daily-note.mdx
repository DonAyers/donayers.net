This week I’ve felt how aggressively the world gets narrated at me.

Not just “here’s what happened,” but *here’s what it means*, *here’s who to blame*, *here’s the posture to take*, *here’s the joke you’re supposed to repeat so everyone knows you’re on the right team*.

A lot of the most viral stories aren’t built to help me understand anything. They’re built to move me.

They’re tuned for my nervous system: make me anxious, then offer certainty. Make me lonely, then offer belonging. Make me tired, then offer a villain so I can turn my fatigue into heat and call it clarity.

And I get it. When life feels expensive and fragile, when the future feels like it keeps narrowing, the clean story is a relief. It gives you something to hold.

But I keep noticing the hidden trade.

If the story gives me instant certainty, it usually wants to take something from me. It wants to shorten my attention span. It wants to make me suspicious of curiosity. It wants to convert other people into props in a drama where my job is to clap, boo, and keep the plot moving.

The narratives that bother me most lately aren’t even the ones that are obviously false. They’re the ones that are *selectively true* — true enough to justify a mood, but shallow enough to keep me from looking at what’s underneath.

Underneath, I hear people trying to say a few basic things.

I hear: I don’t feel safe. I don’t trust the people in charge. I’m working harder for less. I don’t know what the rules are anymore. I’m tired of being lied to. I’m tired of being talked down to. I’m tired of pretending this isn’t scary.

Those are human sentences. They don’t automatically turn into good ideas, and they don’t excuse cruelty. But if we can’t hear them, we end up arguing with armor. We fight the slogan instead of the fear. And when we do that, the only people who win are the ones selling the slogans.

I think about this at work, building software in healthcare.

When a workflow breaks, the fastest story is usually a moral one: someone was careless; someone didn’t follow directions; someone didn’t do their job. That story is emotionally efficient. It gives you a clean culprit and a clean ending.

But the more honest story is usually structural. The language was unclear. The handoff was brittle. The system assumed too much. The “edge case” was someone’s real day. The incentives rewarded speed over care.

That story is harder because it asks for responsibility without the cheap comfort of contempt. It keeps a person in view. It forces you to fix the actual thing.

Public life feels like it’s getting the opposite treatment: we’re drowning in moral stories because they’re faster. They let us skip the slow work of grief, repair, and building anything that would make the future feel wider.

Bad-faith negativity thrives in that environment. It takes a shared, complicated discomfort and offers a simple handle. It doesn’t just say “this is wrong.” It says: *stay angry, stay certain, stay entertained.* It turns humiliation into a hobby and calls it realism.

If I’m honest, part of what I’m working on is noticing how much of that is *tempting*. Not because I want to be mean, but because I want to feel protected.

Contempt feels like armor. Certainty feels like shelter. But neither one is the same thing as seeing clearly.

The small practice I’m trying right now is to watch for the moment I feel recruited. The moment a story makes my hands want to move before my heart does — share it, dunk it, quote it, turn a person into a symbol.

When I notice that impulse, I try to translate the story into a question that keeps me human.

What is this asking me to become? What is it asking me to stop noticing? What fear is it touching in me? If I had to describe the other side in a way they would recognize as fair, could I? What would it look like to name harm without enjoying humiliation?

Sometimes the answers don’t soften anything. Sometimes the answer is still: yes, this is harmful, and it matters.

But the questions help me find a different kind of steadiness — the kind that can hold complexity without going numb, and can care without needing to be cruel.

I don’t have a grand theory of where everything is headed. Mostly I’m trying to stay close to actual people: the ones I love, the ones I work with, the ones I pass on an ordinary day. I’m trying to let that realness correct the stories.

Anyway — that’s where I’m at today. Trying to protect a quieter attention, and trying to keep my clarity from costing me my compassion.
