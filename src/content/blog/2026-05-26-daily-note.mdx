This morning I caught myself reaching for my phone like it was going to tell me what kind of day it was.

Before coffee, I’d already been offered a handful of complete narratives: *here’s what’s happening*, *here’s who did it*, *here’s why it proves your side is right*, *here’s the emotion you should perform so nobody doubts you belong*. The content changes, but the shape is familiar. The story doesn’t just want my attention — it wants to steer my nervous system.

I don’t think the loudest narratives win because they’re true. They win because they’re *efficient*. They turn a complicated world into a sorting problem. They give me a clean villain, a clean tribe, and a clean feeling of being oriented.

And I get why that’s tempting.

A lot of us are tired and a little on edge. Money feels tighter, trust feels thinner, and the future is harder to picture without immediately doing the math. In that state, ambiguity can feel like danger, and certainty can feel like relief — even when it’s wrong.

Bad-faith negativity plays that angle by spiking my heart rate and handing me a target. It sells contempt as “just being honest.”

I’ve been trying to notice what happens in me right before I join in.

It’s usually not a thought — it’s a physical shift. My jaw gets tight. My mind speeds up. I start composing replies. I stop wondering and start recruiting evidence. That’s the tell for me: I don’t feel *curious* anymore. I feel armed.

At work, we have language for this. When something goes wrong in software, the first story is almost always personal and dramatic. Someone was careless. Someone didn’t think. Someone “should have known.” That story spreads fast because it feels emotionally satisfying.

But when you actually want the harm to stop repeating, you have to do something less thrilling: separate observation from interpretation. What do we *know* happened? What are we *assuming* happened? What did the system make likely — the defaults, the missing guardrails, the handoff that only works if everyone is rested and has perfect context? The “edge case” is often just a tired human having a normal day.

Public life right now seems to reward the opposite. The fastest interpretation gets treated like the truest one. The hottest posture gets treated like the most principled one.

So I’m practicing a small translation step, mostly for my own sanity.

When I run into a story that tries to hook me instantly, I try to translate it from a verdict into a human sentence. Underneath the dunk, the slogan, the eye-roll, what is someone trying to say?

Often it’s not that complicated. It’s some version of: I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel respected. I don’t trust the people in charge. I’m working hard and it’s not adding up. I want somebody to admit this is scary. I want to know I won’t be abandoned.

Those sentences don’t automatically lead to good ideas. They don’t excuse cruelty. But they change what I’m arguing with. They bring me back to the actual material the world is made of: people with nervous systems, trying to protect what they love.

And once I’m back there, I can ask questions that feel more honest than a take.

Is this narrative making me more accurate, or just more certain?

Is it asking me to see the whole person, or offering me the cheap comfort of turning them into a symbol?

Is it moving me toward anything repairable — a conversation, a boundary, a small act of help — or is it just selling me the temporary thrill of being above somebody?

I don’t have a neat answer for “what’s really going on.” But it does feel like a fight over attention — whether we can be kept keyed up and sure, because keyed-up people are easier to steer.

Today, my counter-practice is boring on purpose. Fewer hot takes. More full sentences. More willingness to say, “I might be missing something.” More attention to the people in front of me, and less attention to the imaginary audience in my pocket.

Not because the stakes are low. Because the stakes are high, and I don’t want my seriousness to be measured by how hard my heart gets.

I want to stay clear without getting cruel. I want to name what’s harmful without needing a villain to fuel me. And I want to keep a person in view — including myself — even when the loudest stories are begging me to forget there’s a person here at all.
