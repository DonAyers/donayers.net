I’ve been noticing how often the world shows up already captioned.

Not just “here’s what happened,” but “here’s what it *means*,” and “here’s what kind of person you are if you don’t react correctly.” The story arrives with the tone baked in: outrage, mockery, certainty, or the brittle kind of optimism that skips past grief.

It’s efficient. It’s also exhausting.

A lot of the narratives spinning right now feel less like an attempt to describe reality and more like an attempt to *sort* people. Who’s in. Who’s out. Who gets to be treated as a full person and who gets turned into a symbol.

I don’t think most people wake up wanting to be cruel. I think people wake up wanting to feel safe and not stupid and not alone.

And when life feels expensive, and the future feels harder to picture, and institutions feel like they’re speaking a different language than the one you use at your own kitchen table, the nervous system starts shopping for anything that makes the world feel simpler. A clean narrative can feel like a handrail. Even if it’s a bad one.

What worries me is how easy it is to confuse emotional heat for clarity.

Bad-faith negativity knows exactly how to do this. It doesn’t need to make me wiser — it just needs to make me *sure*. It needs me to stop asking questions and start performing membership: laugh at the right people, despise the right people, repeat the right phrases. Contempt becomes a kind of currency. Certainty becomes a kind of armor.

I’ve been trying to catch the moment right before I put the armor on.

For me it’s physical. My jaw tightens. My shoulders creep up. My mind starts composing replies to an imaginary audience. Curiosity starts to feel like a liability. That’s the moment the story is no longer just information. It’s a steering wheel.

When I can notice it early, I can try something small: I translate the take back into a human sentence.

Underneath the posture, what is someone trying to protect? What are they afraid will happen if they don’t win this argument? What are they asking for, even if they’re asking for it badly?

Sometimes the sentence is simple and painfully ordinary: I don’t trust the people in charge. I can’t afford another surprise. I feel like the rules changed and nobody told me. I’m tired of being told I’m overreacting. I want someone to admit this is scary.

Those sentences don’t magically produce good policy or good behavior. They don’t excuse harm. But they do something important: they pull me back toward the actual material the world is made of — people with nervous systems.

I think about this a lot in software, because the same pattern shows up there, just with less theatrical lighting.

When something breaks, the first story is almost always personal and dramatic. Someone was careless. Someone didn’t think. Someone should have known. That story spreads because it feels satisfying.

But if you actually want the breakage to stop repeating, you end up asking different questions. What were the incentives? What were the defaults? What was the handoff that only works if everyone is rested and has perfect context? Where did the system quietly make the mistake likely?

Public life right now feels like it’s rewarding the opposite of that discipline. The fastest interpretation gets treated like the truest one. The harshest tone gets treated like the most serious one. And the more you treat people as symbols, the easier it is to keep the machine fed.

I don’t have a grand answer for how to fix that.

What I have is a practice I’m trying to keep, mostly for my own sanity: I’m trying to stay slower than the feed. I’m trying to let “I might be missing something” be a normal sentence. I’m trying to ask one more question before I join a pile-on, even when the pile-on is wearing the costume of righteousness.

Sometimes the most honest response to a spinning narrative is just: I don’t know yet, and I’m not going to rent out my empathy until I do.

And sometimes the most human thing you can do is stay close to whatever is actually in front of you — a real conversation, a real neighbor, a real task you can do that helps someone a little. Not as a way to ignore the stakes, but as a way to keep your seriousness from being measured by how hard your heart gets.

I want to stay clear without getting cruel. I want to be able to name what’s harmful without needing a villain to fuel me. I want my attention to be a place people can still be people.

The stories are going to keep coming. Today I’m trying to meet them with fewer verdicts and more full sentences.
