I keep noticing how little of what I’m seeing right now is *information*.

It’s story.

Not even story in the neutral sense — story with a job. A headline, a clip, a screenshot, a thread: they show up with an implied assignment. *Feel this.* *Pick a side.* *Decide who’s disgusting.* *Share it so your people know you’re one of them.*

A lot of the narratives getting spun lately seem designed less to help us understand what’s happening and more to shape what kind of person we become while we’re watching. It’s subtle. If I’m not paying attention, I start tightening up. I get quicker to assume bad faith. Quicker to flatten people into types. Quicker to treat the world like a courtroom where my job is to win.

And the weird part is how good that can feel.

Certainty is a relief when you’re tired. Outrage can feel like energy when you’re depleted. Contempt can feel like protection when you’re scared.

That’s the layer I’m trying to get underneath: not just *what happened*, but *what is this story doing for the people passing it around?* What need is it meeting? What wound is it poking? What does it let us avoid feeling?

Because when I listen carefully, I don’t hear “I want to dunk on someone” so much as I hear: *I don’t feel safe.* *I don’t feel like the rules apply.* *I’m working harder for less.* *I’m tired of being told everything is fine.* *I don’t know who to trust.* *I’m afraid the future is narrowing.*

Those are human sentences. They’re not always *right* in the way they get translated into politics or culture-war slogans, but the sentences themselves are real. And if we can’t hear them, we end up arguing with people’s armor instead of the fear underneath it.

I see the same dynamic at work in software, especially in healthcare. When something breaks, the fast narrative is almost always moral: somebody did something wrong. A user “failed.” A team “dropped the ball.” Someone “should’ve known better.”

But if you slow down, the more accurate story is usually structural: unclear language, a workflow that assumes too much, incentives that reward speed over care, a handoff where responsibility got blurry. In other words: not a villain, but a system doing what systems do.

Public life feels like that right now. We keep getting handed villain stories because they’re emotionally satisfying and easy to share. But the root of a lot of what’s going on seems less like one bad person and more like a bunch of pressure building in a lot of places at once — money pressure, attention pressure, loneliness pressure, status pressure — until everyone’s nervous system is looking for a handle.

Bad-faith negativity thrives in that environment. It takes a complicated, shared discomfort and offers a clean target. It says: you don’t need to grieve what’s been lost, or admit what you’re afraid of, or do the slow work of building anything; you just need to find the right person to blame and then stay angry.

I don’t think anger is always wrong. Sometimes anger is the part of us that still cares about dignity. But I’m trying to notice the difference between anger that points at reality and anger that gets used to shrink my empathy.

One small practice that’s been helping me is to translate the story I’m being sold into a more human question.

When I catch myself thinking, “Of course those people are like that,” I try to swap it for, “What are they protecting?” When I feel the urge to mock someone, I try to ask, “What would it cost me to assume they’re a person having a hard day?” When I feel recruited into certainty, I try to admit, “I might not have enough context for this.”

That doesn’t make me passive. It just keeps me from getting sloppy.

I want to stay clear-eyed without getting cruel. I want to be able to name harm without enjoying humiliation. I want to be able to disagree without turning the other person into a cardboard cutout.

And I keep coming back to a quiet thought: if the loudest narratives right now are trying to turn fear into certainty, maybe one of the most constructive things I can do is refuse the conversion. To let fear be information, not fuel. To stay close to actual people — the ones I love, the ones I work with, the ones I bump into in the course of an ordinary day — and let that reality correct the stories.

Anyway. That’s where I’m at today. I’m trying to pay attention to what a story is doing to my heart before I let it move my hands.
