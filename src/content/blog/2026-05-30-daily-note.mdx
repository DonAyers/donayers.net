This morning I caught myself doing the thing again: reading a headline, then immediately looking around for the “right” reaction.

Not the facts. Not even my own response. The *approved* response.

It’s a small moment, but it says a lot about what it feels like to be alive right now. Events still happen in the world, of course. But the bigger thing that happens — the thing that hits my nervous system first — is the narration.

The narration arrives fast. It arrives with a tone. It arrives with a pre-packed moral: here’s what this *means*, here’s who it’s *about*, here’s who it’s safe to be angry at, here’s what kind of person you are if you hesitate.

And once that machinery is running, it’s hard to tell the difference between clarity and posture.

A posture is emotionally efficient. It turns uncertainty into belonging. If I adopt the posture, I don’t have to sit with the rawness of not knowing. I get to be on a team. I get to feel like I’m doing something by having the correct level of outrage or sarcasm.

I understand why this is so tempting. A lot of us are carrying more than we were built to carry.

Life is expensive. Everyone I know is tired. Trust feels thinner than it used to. Institutions feel wobbly. The future feels less like a plan and more like a long series of guesses. When my body is living in that kind of background noise, a clean story can feel like a handrail.

But clean stories have a cost.

The fastest narratives tend to flatten people into symbols. They convert complicated motives into caricatures that can be traded quickly: villain, hero, idiot, monster. And when people become symbols, contempt becomes easy.

That’s the part I’m trying to notice more carefully: how often the loudest story isn’t trying to make me more accurate — it’s trying to make me more *certain*. And certainty, when it’s offered as a product, almost always comes with an enemy.

When I slow down enough to listen under the talking points, I hear something else going on.

Not always. Sometimes people really are just being cruel.

But often, underneath the heat, I can hear a normal human sentence being made badly. Something like: *I don’t feel safe.* *I don’t trust anyone to tell me the truth.* *I’m working hard and it isn’t adding up.* *I feel invisible.* *I’m scared and I hate that I’m scared.* *Please don’t make me look stupid for not knowing what to do.*

Those sentences don’t automatically produce good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. But they do point closer to the root: a lot of what we’re seeing is anxious people trying to get their hands on some dignity and control, in a world that keeps slipping through their fingers.

I think about this at work because software is merciless about stories.

When something breaks, the first story is usually personal: someone was careless, someone didn’t care, some team dropped the ball. That story travels fast because it feels clean. It also feels good, for a second, because it gives you an outlet.

But if you actually want fewer outages and fewer repeat mistakes, you end up doing something less satisfying: you go looking for context. You look at incentives, handoffs, defaults, ambiguous wording, missing guardrails. You look at what the system made likely — especially for a tired human on a busy day.

That kind of attention is slower, but it produces repair instead of heat.

Public life feels like it’s losing patience for that slowness. Everything wants to be immediate. Everything wants to be a test. Everything wants to turn “are you paying attention?” into “prove you’re on the right side.”

So today I’m trying a small practice that’s mostly about keeping my own heart from hardening.

Before I share the take — before I even fully *adopt* the take — I try to say one honest sentence to myself:

What do I actually know here? What am I guessing? What feeling is this story trying to install in me?

If I can’t answer that, I try to delay. Not forever. Just long enough for my body to come back online. Long enough to remember that my job isn’t to keep up with the feed. My job is to stay in contact with reality.

Reality is still made of people with nervous systems.

Mine included.

And when I can, I try to do something that’s local enough to be true: a real conversation, a real task, a small act of care that doesn’t need an audience. Not as a way of escaping the world, but as a way of staying human inside it.

I don’t have a tidy answer for where things are headed. Mostly I’m trying to hold onto something like: clarity without contempt. Seriousness without spectacle. The ability to name what’s harmful without needing my heart to get harder as proof that I noticed.

The narration will keep spinning.

I’m trying to stay close to the people underneath it — including the person I become when I’m afraid.
