I woke up to that familiar feeling: the world is loud before I’ve even put my feet on the floor.

Not loud in the literal sense. My apartment was quiet. But the feed was already running the day’s scripts. Here’s what matters. Here’s what it *means*. Here’s the correct emotion to have about it. Here’s the villain. Here’s the brave take. Here’s the take that proves you’re not gullible.

I keep thinking about how much of public life right now is really a competition to be the first person to sound certain.

And I get why. A lot of us are walking around with a low-grade hum of anxiety. The future doesn’t feel like a plan; it feels like weather. Institutions feel less trustworthy than they used to. Money is tighter than it should be. Attention is fragmented. When your nervous system is already working overtime, certainty can feel like relief — like you finally found something solid to stand on.

But the kind of certainty that gets rewarded online isn’t usually the kind that makes you more accurate. It’s the kind that makes you more *usable*.

By usable I mean: I’ll share the clip, repeat the line, adopt the posture — and do it quickly enough that it looks like conviction instead of reflex.

And the posture almost always comes with a hidden requirement: someone has to be reduced.

That reduction can look like cruelty, or a joke, or a smug little “obviously.” It can look like treating whole groups of people like a problem to be managed instead of neighbors to be understood. It can look like insisting that hesitation is betrayal and nuance is cowardice. The details change. The shape stays the same.

When I notice myself getting pulled into that shape, the thing that helps me most is a translation exercise.

I try to take the talking point and translate it back into the human sentence underneath it. Not the polished version. Not the version designed to win. The sentence a tired person might be trying to say if they weren’t performing for an audience.

Sometimes it’s: *I don’t feel safe.*

Sometimes it’s: *I’m tired of being lied to.*

Sometimes it’s: *I work hard and it isn’t adding up.*

Sometimes it’s: *Please don’t make me feel stupid for being confused.*

Those sentences don’t magically turn into good ideas. They don’t excuse harm. They don’t mean “both sides” are equally right. But they do something important: they keep me from turning people into symbols.

And symbols are the fuel source for bad-faith negativity.

I see a smaller version of this at work.

When something breaks in a product — a confusing workflow, a support ticket that keeps repeating, a patient-facing step that causes delays — the first story that shows up is usually personal. Someone was careless. Someone didn’t think. That team always does this.

That story travels fast because it feels clean. It gives you a place to put your irritation.

But the story that leads to repair is almost always structural. The UI made the wrong thing the easiest thing. The label assumed context. The process was brittle. The handoff was unclear. The “edge case” wasn’t an edge at all — it was somebody’s real day, under stress, trying to do the right thing quickly.

Public narratives are doing the opposite. They’re constantly encouraging me to stop at the clean, personal story. To enjoy the heat. To feel like being harsh is the same thing as being honest.

Today I’m trying to practice a quieter kind of attention.

Before I forward the take, I’m trying to ask: what do I actually know here?

And: what am I guessing?

And: what feeling is this story trying to install in me?

If the answer is “contempt,” I try to wait. Not forever. Just long enough for my body to come back online. Long enough to remember that I’m not required to turn every event into a performance of certainty.

Then I try to do one small thing that’s local enough to be true — the kind of thing that doesn’t need an audience: answer the text I’ve been avoiding, do a real piece of work, take a walk without consuming anybody’s outrage, ask a question in a conversation instead of delivering a verdict.

None of that fixes the world. But it does change the person I’m becoming while I’m watching it.

The stories will keep coming. Some of them will be thoughtful. A lot of them will be optimized to recruit my fear and sell it back to me as clarity.

I’m trying to keep choosing a different kind of clarity — the kind that doesn’t require me to get mean in order to feel awake. The kind that keeps a person in view, even when the narration is begging me to forget they exist.
