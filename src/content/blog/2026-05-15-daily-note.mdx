Lately it feels like the world isn’t just happening — it’s being *performed*.

Not in a conspiratorial way. More in the plain, human-incentives way: everything gets turned into a story that can travel. A clip becomes a morality play. A headline becomes an identity test. A screenshot becomes proof that *those people* are exactly what we already suspected.

What keeps tripping me up is how often the story arrives with an assignment.

Feel this. Decide fast. Pick the villain. Share it so your people know you’re one of them.

Some days I can feel my own mind cooperating with that pace. It wants the quick snap of certainty. It wants the relief of a clean frame. It wants the little rush of righteousness that comes from being able to say, “See? That’s what’s wrong with everything.”

But when I slow down enough to listen for what people are actually trying to say underneath the talking points, I hear something else.

I hear fear and fatigue.

I hear: *I’m working harder for less.* *I don’t know what the rules are anymore.* *I’m tired of being talked down to.* *I don’t trust the people in charge.* *I’m worried my kids will have fewer options.* *I’m lonely.* *I don’t feel safe.*

Those are human sentences. They don’t automatically turn into good politics or good policy or good culture. But they’re real. And if we can’t hear them, we end up arguing with armor. We fight the slogan instead of the need underneath it.

Bad-faith negativity thrives in that gap.

It takes a shared discomfort and offers a clean target. It says you don’t need to grieve, you don’t need to admit uncertainty, you don’t need to ask hard questions about incentives or structures or your own side’s blind spots. You just need to stay angry, stay certain, and keep the story moving.

The part that matters to me isn’t just whether the story is “true.” It’s what the story is doing to me while I’m holding it. Is it making me more careful? Or is it making me smaller?

I think about this a lot at work, building software in healthcare.

When something goes wrong in a workflow, the fastest story is usually a moral one: someone messed up. A user didn’t follow directions. A team didn’t think it through. There’s comfort in that kind of narrative because it gives you a villain and a clean ending.

But when you actually look, the more accurate story is usually structural: the language was unclear, the handoff was brittle, the system assumed too much, the incentives rewarded speed over care, the “edge case” was someone’s real day.

And when I choose the structural story, it doesn’t let anyone off the hook — it just makes the work more honest. It keeps a person in view.

Public life feels like it’s getting the opposite treatment. We keep getting handed villain stories because they’re emotionally efficient. They let us skip the slow work of holding complexity without going numb. They let us convert fear into certainty, and certainty into contempt.

I don’t want to float above it. I don’t want to become passive or “above the fray.” I just don’t want my attention shaped by narratives that require me to get cruel in order to feel clear.

So I’m trying a small practice.

When I feel recruited — when I feel that internal click of “oh, I know exactly what this is” — I try to translate the story into a question that keeps a human on the other side.

What are they protecting? What are they afraid of? What is the system optimizing for? What would it cost me to assume there’s a real person under the posture?

Sometimes the answer is still: yes, this is wrong, and it matters.

But I’m learning to watch for the difference between naming harm and enjoying humiliation. Between clarity and heat. Between anger that points at reality and anger that’s just a place to dump my own anxiety.

If I have any hope for the direction we’re going, it’s not a prediction. It’s a choice I can repeat.

To stay close to actual people. To take a breath before I let a story move my hands. To hold my certainty a little more lightly. To keep asking questions that make me more precise instead of more contemptuous.

Anyway — that’s where I’m at today. I’m trying to be honest about what’s scary without letting the fear turn into a personality.
