I’ve been noticing how much of “what’s going on” right now is less about events and more about interpretation.

Something happens, and within minutes the world offers me a finished meaning: who’s to blame, what it proves, which side it puts me on, what I’m allowed to feel. The whole thing arrives like a kit. All I have to do is assemble it and post the completed model.

And I get why that’s appealing.

Life feels tight for a lot of people. Trust feels thin. Every system that’s supposed to be steady — money, institutions, workplaces, relationships — has this background hum of strain. When the ground feels like it might move, a clean story is a kind of handrail. It says: *you don’t have to sit in uncertainty; just grab this certainty instead.*

But the stories that show up with the most confidence often want something from me.

They want my nervous system — my adrenaline, my disgust, my sense of righteous speed. They want me to stop being a person and start being a role: the one who dunks, the one who sneers, the one who “just tells it like it is.”

I don’t think the problem is emotion. The problem is when a narrative is engineered to *use* it — to keep it hot and pointed, and keep it from cooling into understanding.

Underneath so many of the loud talking points, I keep hearing the same quieter sentences.

I hear: I’m scared. I feel disposable. I don’t think the people in charge are telling me the truth. I’m working harder and it’s not adding up. I don’t know what rules I’m supposed to trust. I want my life to feel like it’s moving toward something, not just surviving another cycle.

Those are human sentences. And when we can’t hear them, we end up arguing with armor. We fight the meme version of a person instead of the actual person.

I see a small version of this dynamic in my own work.

When something breaks in healthcare software, the fastest explanation is usually a character judgment: the user didn’t read, the team didn’t think, someone messed up. It’s emotionally efficient because it gives you a culprit and lets you move on.

But the more useful explanation is usually structural. The words were unclear. The handoff was brittle. The system assumed too much context. The “edge case” was somebody’s real day — somebody tired, trying to do the right thing quickly.

When I remember that, I don’t get softer about fixing the problem. I get more precise. And the fix tends to last.

Public life feels like it’s being trained in the opposite direction: less patience for structural thinking, more appetite for moral theater.

The kind of negativity that worries me most isn’t simple disagreement. It’s the bad-faith version that treats cruelty as honesty and humiliation as proof. It tells you nothing can be built or repaired — so the only “realistic” move is to stay cynical and keep your guard up.

If I’m honest, that posture can feel like protection. If I expect the worst, I won’t be surprised. If I stay contemptuous, I won’t be vulnerable.

But cynicism isn’t the same thing as clear-eyed. It’s just another way of giving up without admitting you’re grieving.

So I’ve been trying a smaller, quieter practice.

When a story shows up and I can feel it recruiting me — when my hands want to move faster than my heart — I try to slow down long enough to ask: what is this asking me to become? What part of me is it trying to hijack? What fear is it touching?

And then I try to translate the narrative back into the human sentence underneath it.

Not to excuse harm. Not to pretend everything is fine. Just to keep reality in view: a world made of people with nervous systems, histories, bills, kids, losses, hopes. People who are often clumsy about what they’re asking for.

I don’t have a grand answer for where everything is headed. Mostly I’m trying to keep my attention from becoming a weapon. I want the clarity that makes me more accurate, not the certainty that makes me mean.

Today, that feels like enough: refuse the conversion of fear into contempt, and keep making room for the slow questions that let me stay human inside whatever comes next.
