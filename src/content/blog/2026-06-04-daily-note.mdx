I keep noticing how many of the stories floating around right now have the same hidden demand: *be angry, and be sure.*

The details rotate — politics, money, institutions, whatever the headline of the hour is — but the posture stays familiar. If you’re not instantly certain, you’re naive. If you’re not instantly furious, you don’t care. If you ask for context, you’re “making excuses.” If you admit you don’t know, you’re weak.

What I’m bumping into, though, is that the posture doesn’t actually make me more informed. It makes me more *usable*.

It’s easy to mistake cynicism for clarity, because cynicism comes with an answer already attached. It tells me the root of everything is selfishness. It tells me people can’t change. It tells me the system is designed to hurt you on purpose. It tells me there’s no point in trying, except maybe to mock the people who still believe in anything.

And sometimes cynicism is pointing at something real. People do lie. Incentives do warp everything they touch. Power does protect itself. I’m not interested in pretending the world is nicer than it is.

But I’m trying to watch what cynicism does to my attention.

When I’m cynical, I stop listening for what’s actually being said. I listen for the part I can dismiss. I turn human beings into types. I read every mistake as a confession. I treat every disagreement as a moral flaw. It feels sharp in the moment. It also makes the world smaller and my heart harder.

This showed up for me at work yesterday in a way that felt almost embarrassingly literal.

A support issue came in about a workflow that had been confusing people. The first explanation making the rounds was the usual one: “Users don’t read.” It’s a clean story. It lets everyone feel competent and a little superior. It also makes the problem unsolvable, because if the root cause is “people are dumb,” the only fix is scolding.

Then someone took ten minutes to watch a screen recording. The label was ambiguous. The “primary” action looked secondary. The system did exactly what it was designed to make likely: move a tired person through the fastest-looking path.

Once we could see that, the mood shifted. Not because everyone became saintly, but because the story got more accurate. We moved from contempt to repair.

Public narratives keep trying to pull me in the opposite direction. They keep offering me contempt as a substitute for understanding.

One of the strangest things about the internet right now is how often it tries to sell me a feeling as if it were a fact. Not just “here’s what happened,” but “here’s the only acceptable way to *be* about what happened.” The feeling is usually some blend of disgust and certainty. If I accept it quickly enough, I get to feel like I’m one of the people who “gets it.”

When I slow down, I can usually find a more human sentence underneath the performance.

Sometimes it’s: *I don’t trust anyone to tell me the truth.*

Sometimes it’s: *I’m working hard and it isn’t adding up.*

Sometimes it’s: *I’m scared, and I don’t know what to do with that.*

Those sentences don’t magically turn into good conclusions. They don’t excuse cruelty. But they do change the temperature in the room. They make it harder to use people as props.

This morning I took a short walk before checking the news. Just a loop around my neighborhood. The usual stuff: someone watering a small patch of plants, a couple of kids arguing about whose turn it was, a guy on a porch nodding like we’d known each other for years. The offline world still has friction and injustice, obviously — but it also has faces. It has moments where nobody is auditioning.

That’s the perspective I’m trying to carry back into the louder channels.

Not “everything is fine.” Not “nothing matters.” Just a quieter question: what would it look like to see clearly without turning cold?

For me, today, it probably looks like speaking in full sentences. Admitting what I don’t know before I broadcast what I feel. Staying curious about incentives without assuming every person is a villain. Doing one local thing that’s actually real — a message, a task, a small act of care — so my whole day isn’t spent reacting to a story designed to keep me raw.

The narratives will keep spinning. Some of them will even be right.

I’m trying to remember that being right isn’t the only goal. Staying human is a goal too.
